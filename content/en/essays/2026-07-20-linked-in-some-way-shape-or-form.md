On Monday the State Department released a hundred-page report titled «Cuba: The Capital of 21st Century Communism», introduced by Secretary Marco Rubio with the declaration that the American people deserve to know the full history of Cuban espionage and subversion. The headline the document is built to generate — and the only part of it most people will ever encounter — is that Havana stands behind the American left: behind Black Lives Matter, behind the Democratic Socialists of America, behind Antifa, behind the George Floyd protests, behind campus activism. That headline is not what the report demonstrates. It is what the report insinuates, through a rhetorical architecture worth taking apart precisely because so few of its readers will open the document far enough to see it.

The report is really two documents stapled together. The first is a genuinely documented history: Ana Belén Montes at the Defense Intelligence Agency, Víctor Manuel Rocha's half-century as a Cuban agent inside the foreign-policy establishment, Kendall Myers at the State Department, the Venceremos Brigade's trips to the island since 1969, Cuba's Cold War training of guerrillas and its asylum for American fugitives, Assata Shakur most famously among them. Almost none of this is new; nearly all of it was adjudicated in courtrooms, congressional hearings, and declassified files decades ago, and the report's footnotes lead back to 1973 hearing transcripts and published journalism rather than to fresh intelligence. The second document is a set of claims about the present — that today's progressive organizations constitute «one of the single largest, most sophisticated and dangerous vectors for hostile foreign influence in modern American history». The stapling is the entire method: the evidentiary weight of the first document is meant to be felt, without ever being transferred, beneath the second.

The seam shows in a single sentence, the one that will be quoted everywhere and scrutinized nowhere:

> Many of the most significant upheavals in recent American political history — from the George Floyd riots to the rise of Antifa to the explosion of pro-terrorist activism on American college campuses — can be linked, in some way, shape, or form, to Cuban influence.

Read it slowly. «Linked, in some way, shape, or form» is not a finding; it is an insurance policy. A claim hedged this thoroughly cannot be false, because it cannot be tested. By the same standard, the American Revolution can be linked, in some way, shape, or form, to French influence, and the Cold War American right to émigré European anticommunism. Intellectual genealogy is real — ideas travel, and Havana spent decades cultivating exactly this traffic — but genealogy is not operation, and influence is not control. The sentence performs causation while committing to none, which is the signature not of intelligence analysis but of prosecution by atmosphere.

The report concedes the point itself, in the section devoted to its most politically useful target. The Democratic Socialists of America, it acknowledges, «was not founded as a Cuban intelligence or influence front» and is not controlled by Cuban agents «per se»; its offense is that its convictions now coincide with Havana's interests «of its own accord». Strip the ominous register and what remains is this: an American membership organization holds a policy position — normalization, an end to the embargo — that a foreign government also holds, announces its delegations publicly, and meets openly with officials of a state the United States maintains relations with grievances against. The report treats the openness itself as sinister, lingering on five-star hotels and fine dining as though the quality of the accommodations were the quality of the evidence. But opposition to the embargo is not a fringe position smuggled in from abroad; it has been the position of overwhelming majorities in the UN General Assembly for three decades and of consistent majorities of Americans in polling. When agreement with a foreign government's stated preference becomes the evidentiary standard for foreign subversion, the standard indicts half of any democracy at any moment.

The Black Lives Matter passage works the same conversion. The report states that BLM leaders «firmly sided with the Cuban government over the protestors» during the July 2021 demonstrations. What the organization actually published was a statement blaming U.S. policy for the island's economic distress and praising Cuba's historical solidarity with Black liberation movements — a statement legitimately criticized at the time, including from the left, for its silence on the Cuban state's repression of the largely Afro-Cuban protesters filling the streets. A one-sided statement is a political failure; it is not coordination with a foreign intelligence service. The report's method dissolves that distinction on purpose, because the distinction is precisely what separates bad opinions, which a free society metabolizes, from foreign agency, which a state may lawfully act against.

There is also a structural contradiction the report cannot resolve because it needs both halves. Cuba is described, in the same document, as a chronically failing state whose economy «teeters on the brink of collapse» — and as the ideological capital of a hemispheric network so potent that it steers the most significant upheavals in American political life. The island currently endures rolling blackouts, fuel rationing, and an exodus of roughly a quarter of its population since 2021; its principal patron was decapitated by U.S. forces in January and its oil imports strangled by blockade since. A regime this prostrate cannot simultaneously be the puppeteer of American civil society. The contradiction is not carelessness. The paranoid style always requires an enemy weak enough to be toppled and omnipotent enough to justify the toppling, and the report supplies both on demand.

Which brings us to timing, the least deniable fact about the document. It arrives seven months into a pressure campaign that has included the capture of Nicolás Maduro, a naval blockade of Cuba's oil lifeline, a declared national emergency naming the island an extraordinary threat, more than two hundred sanctions, and the president's public warnings — «make a deal, before it is too late» in January, and last week, on Fox News, that «a lot of things are going to happen in Cuba over the next several months». Governments preparing coercive action produce papers like this one; it is a genre with a lineage of its own, from the 1981 White Paper on El Salvador to the dossiers of 2002. The genre's function is not to inform but to predicate — to place on the record, before the action, a document that can later be cited as its justification.

And the report carries a second function, domestic and unprecedented in its explicitness: it is a State Department publication that names the sitting administration's political opponents — DSA, whose members now include the mayor of New York; BLM; anti-ICE organizers; the National Lawyers Guild; Code Pink — as nodes of hostile foreign influence. Whatever one thinks of any of these organizations, the maneuver itself should be recognized for what it is. Designating domestic dissent as the instrument of a foreign enemy is among the oldest reflexes of state power, and the United States has its own history with it, from the Palmer raids through COINTELPRO. A foreign-policy document that doubles as a domestic enemies list is not describing subversion. It is practicing a politics for which subversion is the permission slip.

None of this requires pretending the Cuban state is innocent of its history or gentle with its people. The espionage was real; the asylum for fugitives was real; the repression of the 2021 protesters was real and continues. But the report's conclusion does not follow from its evidence, and its authors appear to know it — the hedge at its center is the confession. For the reader who will only ever see the headline, the distillation is this: a government preparing to act against Cuba has published a hundred pages in which everything verified is old and everything new is unverifiable, and has asked the phrase «in some way, shape, or form» to carry the difference.
