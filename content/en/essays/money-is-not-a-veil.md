There is a quiet assumption buried in most introductory economics, and almost everything follows from it. The assumption is that money is a *veil*: a convenient cloth thrown over what is really a barter economy, where goods trade for goods and prices, output, and jobs are settled first, in "real" terms, with money added afterward as a counting device. On this picture the economy is fundamentally stable. Markets clear, resources find their most efficient use, and full employment is the normal resting state. Crises, when they come, are shocks from outside—a war, a pandemic, a bad harvest—that knock a sound machine off balance until it rights itself.

There is an older and more heretical tradition that flatly denies this. Its members do not form a single school; they argue with each other as much as with the orthodoxy, and they range politically from revolutionary to technocratic. But they share one conviction: a capitalist economy is organized around money from the ground up, and *because* it is, instability is not an accident that befalls it from outside. Instability is something the system generates from within, in the ordinary course of doing what it does. Booms produce the conditions for busts. Calm breeds the risk-taking that ends calm. The cloth is not a veil over the machine; the cloth *is* the machine.

This essay is a guided tour of that tradition—Marx, Fisher, Keynes, Kalecki, Schumpeter, Minsky, Leijonhufvud, Henwood, and Mehrling—written in plain language, including the places where the orthodoxy lands real blows in return. And because ideas are easiest to judge against the world in front of us, I read it throughout against the forces actually moving the global economy in the middle of 2026: an extraordinary investment boom in artificial intelligence, a mountain of public and private debt, and a fresh energy shock out of a Middle East war.

## The first move: money is not a veil

Start with the idea that gives the tradition its name. In the orthodox story, you produce in order to consume: you bring goods to market, sell them, and use the proceeds to buy other goods. Money is just the lubricant. Marx put a different formula at the centre of capitalism, and it is worth learning because everything hinges on it. He wrote it as **M–C–M′**. In plain English: you start with *money* (M), you buy *commodities* (C)—labour, machines, materials—you produce and sell, and you end up with *more money* (M′) than you began with. The little prime mark is the whole point. The purpose of the exercise is not the goods; the goods are a detour. The purpose is to end the cycle with a larger pile of money than you started with. Production is undertaken for profit, and if the numbers don't promise a profit, production simply doesn't happen—however many people want the goods and however many workers want the jobs.

Once you see capitalism this way, unemployment stops being a puzzle. A firm will leave factories idle and workers unhired if hiring them won't return more money than it costs. Demand for things people need can sit unmet next to people who need work, because the system is not coordinating needs with capacities; it is coordinating money with the prospect of more money.

This is also where the tradition picks its first fight with the textbook. Open any principles course and you meet the formula **Y = C + I + G + NX**: national output equals what households spend (C, consumption), plus what businesses spend on investment (I), plus government spending (G), plus the net of exports over imports (NX). This is true. It is also nearly empty, because it is an accounting identity—a way of *adding up* spending after the fact, not an explanation of what makes any of those pieces rise or fall. The interesting questions live underneath it: what governs investment, why spending can collapse, why the pieces don't automatically sum to full employment. Treating the identity as the substance of Keynes is a quiet way of defanging him.

## The fact that breaks the model: radical uncertainty

Why can't the pieces be trusted to sum to full employment on their own? Because the decisions that drive the economy are decisions about a future nobody can know.

The economist Frank Knight drew the distinction this tradition lives on. **Risk** is a gamble with known odds—a roulette wheel, a dice game. You don't know the next outcome, but you know the full list of outcomes and their probabilities, and you can insure against them. **Uncertainty** is something else entirely: not knowing the odds at all, or even what game is being played. Will a new technology pay off? Will there be a war in five years? What will the price of energy be in 1947, or in 2030? There is no actuarial table for these. As Keynes put it, on such questions "we simply do not know." The future is not a known wheel of fortune; it is genuinely open.

This sounds like a small philosophical point. It is actually a wrecking ball, because the dominant modern models assume agents form *rational expectations*—that people behave as if they know the true probability distribution of the future and are right on average. If the future has no fixed distribution to be right about, that assumption has nothing to stand on. You cannot be rational about odds that don't exist.

So how do people act at all? Keynes's answer was that we lean on three crutches, each of which is also a fault line. We lean on **convention**: the working assumption that things will carry on roughly as they are unless we have specific reason to think otherwise. We lean on **animal spirits**: the spontaneous urge to act that fills the gap where calculation runs out—"if animal spirits are dimmed," Keynes wrote, "enterprise will fade and die." And we lean on **money itself** as a refuge. This is the deep function of money beyond buying things: holding cash is a way of keeping your options open against a future you can't foresee, a way of *not committing*. Keynes called the urge to hold wealth in ready, spendable form **liquidity preference**, and he saw it as a barometer of fear. When confidence in the future falls, people scramble to hold money rather than assets—and that scramble, multiplied across an economy, is exactly how a downturn deepens.

## Why the economy can get stuck

Keynes's central claim was the **principle of effective demand**: spending creates output and jobs, not the other way around. If households and firms collectively decide to spend less—to save more, to hoard cash, to postpone investment—then output and employment fall, and there is no automatic mechanism that brings them back. The economy can settle into a lasting slump that clears no market's idea of "equilibrium" except a miserable one with millions out of work.

Here the orthodox reply is that this only happens because wages and prices are "sticky"—if only they fell fast enough, everything would clear. It is worth being blunt that **this is not Keynes's argument, and the economist who showed why was Axel Leijonhufvud.** In a careful 1968 reading, he separated "the economics of Keynes" from the watered-down "Keynesian" textbook that followed. Keynes's point was not that prices are glued in place. It was that a market economy has no auctioneer standing in the middle calling out the prices that would make everything balance. Trades happen at the "wrong" prices; signals get crossed; quantities (output, hiring) often adjust *faster* than prices, so firms respond to weak sales by firing people before prices have any chance to do their supposed magic. The result is a **coordination failure**—a traffic jam, not a sticky gear—and the economy can sit in it. Indeed, Keynes argued in his most pointed chapter that cutting wages in a slump could make things *worse*, by lowering incomes and raising the real weight of debt.

The Polish economist **Michał Kalecki** reached the same destination as Keynes by a different road, and added a sharper edge. He noticed something counterintuitive about profits. In the economy as a whole, where do business profits come from? Not, in aggregate, from selling to consumers who are mostly other firms' workers. They come, Kalecki showed, largely from what businesses *themselves* spend—on investment and on their owners' own consumption. Hence his famous compression: **"workers spend what they get, capitalists get what they spend."** Investment drives profits, rather than profits simply rewarding past thrift. And investment is the most volatile, mood-driven, uncertainty-haunted decision in the whole system—which is precisely why the system lurches.

Kalecki then drew a conclusion few orthodox economists would touch. In a 1943 essay, he argued that permanent full employment is technically achievable but politically resisted by business—not for economic reasons but because full employment removes the disciplining fear of "the sack." When workers can't be easily replaced, their bargaining power rises and the boss's authority weakens. So the cycle of boom and slump is partly *engineered*, or at least tolerated, as a tool of social control. You don't have to accept the whole argument to feel its relevance every time a central banker frets, in public, that the labour market is "too tight."

## The cycle of debt: Fisher and Minsky

If uncertainty explains why confidence is fragile, two thinkers explain how that fragility turns into a financial avalanche.

**Irving Fisher** had a personal stake in the question: he lost his fortune in the 1929 crash, weeks after assuring the public that stocks had reached a permanently high plateau. Out of that ruin he built the **debt-deflation** theory. Picture a downturn in which everyone is over-indebted and tries to do the prudent thing at once—sell assets, cut spending, pay down debt. The trouble is that mass selling drives prices down, and falling prices make every remaining dollar of debt *heavier* in real terms. So the harder everyone tries to escape their debt, the deeper they sink into it. Fisher's grim paradox: the more the debtors pay, the more they owe. What looks rational for each household becomes catastrophic for all of them—a downward spiral of selling, falling prices, falling profits, falling output, and rising real debt burdens.

**Hyman Minsky** turned this into a theory of the whole cycle, and his one-line summary is the motto of the tradition: **stability is destabilizing.** His mechanism is almost behavioural. When times are calm and good, yesterday's cautious bets all pay off, so caution comes to look like money left on the table. Lenders and borrowers gradually relax their guard. Minsky sorted the financing of any business or household into three rungs. A **hedge** unit earns enough to cover both its interest and its principal—it can pay its debts from its income. A **speculative** unit earns enough to cover the interest but not the principal, so it must keep rolling the debt over. A **Ponzi** unit earns enough to cover neither, and survives only as long as asset prices keep rising so it can borrow against the gains. The deep point is that a long calm pushes the *whole economy* up these rungs, from hedge toward speculative toward Ponzi, without anyone deciding to be reckless. Each step looks reasonable; the system as a whole becomes a tower of matchsticks. Then a small disappointment—a missed payment, a tick up in interest rates—knocks one matchstick, and the rush to sell begins. That tipping point now has a name: the **Minsky moment.**

One honest caveat, which the tradition's own enthusiasts often skip: Minsky did not *prove* any of this. He offered a compelling argument with strong historical resonance, describing a *tendency* that policy can interrupt—not an iron law that guarantees a crash on schedule. Keep that distinction; we will need it when the critics arrive.

## The dynamo and its discontents: Schumpeter

So far the tradition can sound relentlessly gloomy. **Joseph Schumpeter** supplies the engine of motion, and he was no pessimist about it. For Schumpeter the essence of capitalism is **creative destruction**: the perpetual gale in which new products, methods, and firms destroy the old. The hero is the entrepreneur, and—crucially for a tradition about money—the entrepreneur is financed by **credit**. Banks create new purchasing power out of nothing and hand it to innovators to assemble "new combinations" before the resources exist to pay for them. Money here is not a veil; it is the funding that calls the future into being. Schumpeter also saw business cycles as driven by *clusters* of innovation: breakthroughs arrive in bunches, investment surges to exploit them, and the surge eventually exhausts itself.

But it would be dishonest to file Schumpeter neatly alongside Keynes and Minsky, and this is where the "tradition" shows its seams. Schumpeter was a conservative who thought downturns had a *function*—they cleared away the deadwood, the failed firms and bad bets, and any attempt to prop them up merely preserved waste. He would have looked at the others' favourite remedy—governments and central banks rushing to backstop a failing system—and called it a way of cancelling the cleanse. When you read that "this tradition believes instability is built in," remember that its members violently disagree about whether that is a disease to be cured or a feature to be left alone.

## The plumbing and the politics: Mehrling and Henwood

Two more figures fill in the financial machinery, and they too sit at opposite poles.

**Perry Mehrling** offers what he calls the **money view**, and its central image is a hierarchy. Not all money is equal: at the top sits the most ultimate, unquestioned form (central-bank money, gold historically), and below it a pyramid of credit—bank deposits, securities, IOUs—each layer promising to convert into the layer above. In calm times the layers trade as if equivalent. In a panic everyone tries to climb the pyramid at once, demanding the real thing, and the promises lower down collapse. Mehrling's sharpest distinction is between **solvency** and **liquidity**. To be solvent is to own more than you owe. To be liquid is to be able to get cash *right now*. The two are not the same, and the gap kills: a solvent firm whose assets are sound but unsellable today can die this afternoon for lack of cash, as a trader's grim proverb has it—liquidity kills you quick. From this follows Mehrling's view of the modern central bank as a **dealer of last resort**: in a crisis its job is not merely to lend to solvent banks but to step into frozen markets and *make a market*, buying what no one else will, so the pyramid does not implode.

**Doug Henwood** stands on the other side. A sharp critic of Wall Street, he draws a hard line between the **productive** economy, where things are actually made and services rendered, and the **speculative** one, where finance largely shuffles claims and extracts a cut. In his telling, finance is less the helpful plumbing Mehrling describes and more a mechanism of **wealth extraction**, tilting the economy toward short-term myopia and feeding inequality. The tension with Mehrling is real and unresolved: is modern finance essential infrastructure that occasionally needs rescuing, or a parasitic overlay that has captured too much of the surplus? The tradition contains both answers and does not reconcile them.

## 2026: the theory meets the world

Lay all of this over the global economy as it actually stands in mid-2026, and the fit is uncomfortably good.

**The AI boom is a Schumpeter–Minsky–Keynes case study in real time.** Begin with Schumpeter: artificial intelligence is exactly the kind of clustered, general-purpose innovation that triggers an investment surge, financed by a torrent of credit and equity poured in ahead of the revenues that are supposed to justify it. The scale is staggering. The largest US technology firms are on course to spend north of six hundred billion dollars on AI infrastructure in 2026 alone, with total global AI spending estimated in the trillions, according to industry trackers and figures compiled by analysts at Gartner and others. Allianz's researchers note that this has pushed the big spenders' capital expenditure toward roughly a third of their revenue and driven their combined free cash flow negative for the first time in over three decades—firms are deliberately spending far more than they earn, on the conviction that the payoff will come.

Now add Minsky, and the picture darkens. The financing of this boom has the recursive, self-referential quality his theory predicts. Analysts at Man Group describe the AI build-out as one of the most debt-intensive capital cycles in modern business history and point to a reflexive loop: rising valuations justify heavier spending, heavier spending is read as a signal of explosive future demand, and that signal pushes valuations higher still—until, as they put it, the revenue curve fails to steepen in time and the loop breaks. Investigations by Bloomberg and others have mapped the "circular deals" tying the major players together, where the same dollars cycle between chipmaker, cloud provider, and AI lab, each transaction booked as someone's revenue. Lending to AI-related firms by private-credit funds—the lightly regulated shadow-banking corner Minsky would have watched like a hawk—has grown from near zero to over two hundred billion dollars, by the Bank for International Settlements' reckoning, and could multiply by the end of the decade. Most tellingly, the Federal Reserve in 2026 formally listed AI as one of the top systemic risks to financial stability. And the Keynesian hinge is the productivity question: despite the hundreds of billions spent, multiple analyses report no clearly measurable boost to economic output yet. If investors collectively *reassess* whether the returns will ever arrive—if the convention breaks and animal spirits dim—Keynes's logic says investment can fall off a cliff, dragging demand down with it.

**But intellectual honesty requires the other side, and it is strong.** A serious camp argues this is a boom, not a bubble, and they marshal exactly the kind of evidence the orthodoxy would emphasize. Analysts at Fidelity point out that the giants are largely funding this from their own cash flow rather than borrowing, with the broad market's capital-spending-to-cash-flow ratio sitting below one—against roughly four times that at the peak of the dot-com mania in 2000. The Nasdaq-100 trades at a price-to-earnings multiple around 33, rich but nothing like the 60 of March 2000. Much of the spending is on physical infrastructure—data centres, power, chips—that can in principle be redeployed if any one firm's bet fails. And as observers at INSEAD note, the market has repeatedly *punished* aggressive spending announcements rather than cheering them, which is not the behaviour of a crowd blindly buying a vision. The instability lens tells you where to look—at the financing structure, at the gap between asset prices and the cash flows meant to validate them—but it does not, by itself, tell you which way this particular bet breaks. That ambiguity is the point, and we will come back to it as a criticism.

**The energy shock is the perfect test of "shock versus fragility."** In 2026 a war in the Middle East disrupted energy supply—damaged infrastructure, shipping through the Strait of Hormuz in question—and sent oil and gas prices sharply higher. The IMF, World Bank, and OECD all marked global growth down and inflation up as a result, and this looks, on its face, exactly like the orthodox story: an external shock hitting a sound machine. Fair enough; sometimes lightning really does strike from outside, and the tradition should not pretend every misfortune is endogenous. But its deeper claim is about the *kindling*. A shock detonates whatever fragility has already been built up. An energy spike lands very differently on an economy with low debt and ample policy room than on one where, as the IMF now warns, public debt has piled up to levels that themselves threaten financial stability. Which brings us to the debt.

**The debt overhang vindicates Fisher, Minsky, and Mehrling at once.** US government debt has climbed to roughly 124% of GDP with deficits near 6%, and the IMF has taken the unusual step of describing the US fiscal trajectory as a growing financial-stability *tail risk* for the whole world; global sovereign debt is projected to exceed 100% of GDP before the decade is out. Investors are demanding more compensation to hold long-term government bonds and tilting toward shorter maturities—a quiet vote of reduced confidence. And the central bank is behaving precisely as Mehrling's theory says it must: the Fed has halted the shrinking of its balance sheet, strengthened its standing facilities for lending against collateral, and resumed buying assets to keep markets liquid—dealer-of-last-resort functions, normalized. Meanwhile the first cracks in private credit have appeared, with defaults at firms like Tricolor and First Brands prompting worries about lax underwriting in exactly the opaque corner the tradition distrusts. Stability had bred complacency; complacency built fragility; fragility is now being managed by an ever-more-present official backstop.

That backstop is itself the tradition's deepest internal argument, live in 2026. Minsky's prescription—a "Big Bank" and "Big Government" putting a floor under the system—has plainly worked to prevent collapse, repeatedly, since 2008. But Schumpeter's objection bites: each rescue that prevents the cleanse arguably preserves the rot and teaches everyone that risk will be socialized, breeding a larger fragility next time. Henwood's objection bites too: the backstop tends to protect asset-holders first, deepening the inequality finance already drives. The tradition diagnoses the disease and then disagrees, fundamentally, about whether the cure is medicine or addiction.

## Where the critics are right

A tour that only flattered these thinkers would betray them. The orthodoxy and the tradition's own internal skeptics land real punches, and the honest reader should feel them.

The sharpest is **unfalsifiability**. A theory that explains every crisis after the fact, but cannot tell you *when* the next one will hit, is closer to a worldview than a science. "Stability is destabilizing" can rationalize any outcome: if the crash comes, the theory is vindicated; if it doesn't, fragility is simply still building. Commentators have been calling a "Minsky moment" on one boom or another for years, and the AI cycle has been pronounced a bubble repeatedly while continuing to climb. Forecasting crises is genuinely hard, and this tradition's record at *timing* is poor—it tends to predict far more crashes than actually occur, the perennial affliction of the perma-bear. Being early, in markets, is indistinguishable from being wrong.

The orthodox rejoinder on **uncertainty** also has teeth. If the future truly has no calculable structure, then the theory offers diagnosis but few operating instructions—you cannot build a usable policy rule on "animal spirits" and "conventions." And the boom-not-bubble evidence is a live rebuke: agents in 2026 are visibly *policing* cash flows, punishing overspending, pricing risk—behaving a good deal more like the orthodoxy's calculating agents than like a uncomprehending herd.

Then there is the **inflation blind spot**, the tradition's most awkward recent failure. Its instincts run toward demand: spend more, put fiscal floors under the economy, treat unemployment as the central evil. The inflation that erupted after 2021, and the stubborn core inflation and energy-driven price pressure still visible in 2026, are a reminder that the *supply* side and price stability matter in ways a pure demand-deficiency story underweights. The boldest modern descendant of this lineage, Modern Monetary Theory—which argues that a government issuing its own currency is constrained only by real resources, not by finance—was stress-tested hard by that inflation, and "deficits don't matter until they suddenly do" is a fair epitaph for the overreach.

The **Marxian core** carries its own contested baggage. The labour theory of value and the "law" of the falling rate of profit have been argued over for a century—the rate of profit has so many offsetting tendencies that the law becomes nearly impossible to test, and Marx's anticipated final breakdown has stood the system up many times without arriving. Capitalism has proven more adaptive, and more capable of reinventing itself, than its obituarists expected.

And finally, the **incoherence of the "school"** is a criticism in itself. I have tried to flag the seams as we went—Marx locating crisis in production while Minsky locates it in financial psychology; Henwood treating finance as parasite while Mehrling treats it as plumbing; Schumpeter wanting the fire to burn while Minsky rushes to put it out. To present these as one tradition is to flatter a coherence that isn't there. What they share is a starting point and a set of questions, not a doctrine.

## What survives, and why it matters now

Strip away the overreach and a durable, defensible core remains—and in 2026 it is exactly the core you want.

Money and balance sheets are causal, not a veil: who owes what to whom, and in what form, shapes the real economy directly. The future is genuinely uncertain, so confidence and convention are not psychological footnotes but load-bearing structures that can give way. Financing structures matter, and fragility accumulates quietly during the good times rather than arriving from outside. And a capitalist economy requires institutional scaffolding—lenders and dealers of last resort, fiscal floors—that it does not naturally provide and cannot do without.

This tradition cannot tell you whether the AI boom breaks this year or compounds for another decade. What it can do—what it is *for*—is tell you where to point the flashlight. It says: watch the financing structure, not just the technology. Watch the gap between soaring asset prices and the cash flows that are supposed to justify them. Watch the debt stocks and the thinness of the buffers a shock would land on. Watch who is quietly becoming the backstop for whom, and at what eventual cost. And notice that the very rescues which keep the system upright may be loading the spring for the next lurch.

That is a more modest claim than "the crash is coming," and a more useful one. The world economy in 2026 is not a stable machine waiting to be disturbed. It is a monetary system that, in the ordinary course of doing what it does—chasing M′, financing the new, growing calm and then careless—manufactures its own fragility. Knowing that won't let you time the next crisis. It will keep you from being surprised that there is one.
